🧪 pH: THE NUMBER THAT LIES

A scale designed so that enormous changes look small
0 · ACID7 · NEUTRAL14 · ALKALINE
pH is a number between 0 and 14 that tells you how acidic something is. Low is acid, 7 is neutral, high is alkaline. That much you already know.
What almost nobody is told is how the scale is built — and it is built in a way that makes gigantic changes look like small ones. Press releases rely on this.
Four parts. First you place sixteen everyday things on the scale, watching a counter as you go. Then you learn the one trick for reading pH gaps properly. Then five calculations. Then two newspaper headlines about the same measurement.

THE WHOLE SCALE, WITH THE REAL ION COUNTS
Two columns to look at. Every step of one pH unit divides the hydrogen ions by ten — that's the fourth column. And the last column counts down from pH 0, where there is exactly one mole of hydrogen ions per litre: the anchor the whole scale is built on. By the time you reach the middle of the scale you have divided by ten million, and by the far end, by a hundred trillion.
THE ONE TRICK
Subtract the two pH values. The answer is ten to the power of that.
That's it. Three steps, every time:
StepWorked example: clean rain vs acid rain
1. Write both pH values downclean rain 5.6, acid rain 4.3
2. Subtract, smaller from larger5.6 − 4.3 = 1.3
3. Ten to the power of that101.3 = about 20 times more hydrogen ions

And for a big gap, split it. Say the difference is 5.9. Ten to the power of five is a hundred thousand. Ten to the power of nought point nine is about eight. Multiply them: about eight hundred thousand. No calculator, three seconds.

WHAT YOU NOW KNOW THAT THE TEXTBOOK PAGE DOESN'T SAY
The pH scale is a compression device.
It squeezes a range of a hundred trillion into the numbers nought to fourteen. That is genuinely useful — nobody wants 0.00000000000001 on a graph axis. But it has a side effect worth being permanently suspicious of: it makes every change look small.
So when you see a pH change reported as a decimal, the honest question is never “is 0.1 a big number?” It is: ten to the power of what?
One number to keep. Your tooth enamel starts dissolving at about pH 5.5. Clean, unpolluted rain sits at 5.6. A can of cola sits at about 2.5 — which, as you can now work out in your head, is around a thousand times more acidic than the rain.
