Reveal the explanation

A wave and its reflection overlap. Their electric fields add. At some places they reinforce each other; at others they cancel. That creates a standing wave .

The high-exposure stripes are at antinodes . The gaps contain nodes . Neighbouring antinodes are half a wavelength apart.

Important: the field changes direction, but the locations of strong and weak exposure stay put in this model.

Why doesn’t every material heat the same way?

Microwaves carry a changing electric field . Water molecules have slightly positive and negative ends. They try to turn with the field. Their delayed response transfers energy into the liquid.

But water is not the only absorber. Dissolved charged particles and other ingredients matter too. A material’s composition, shape, and surroundings affect the result.

2.45 GHz is not a special, unique “water resonance” setting.

For the teacher: model boundaries, prompts & sources

What the model deliberately simplifies

  • Parts 1–3 use one ideal standing-wave stripe pattern: intensity is proportional to sin²(2πx/λ). This is not a full solution for a closed metal box at every slider frequency.
  • The displayed 32 cm width is a measuring window. A real cavity supports more complicated three-dimensional patterns with boundary constraints.
  • Food does not modify the field here. Real food can modify it.
  • Changing frequency changes wavelength and spacing. We hold ordinary-food absorption fixed to isolate that idea; real absorption and penetration also depend on frequency and the material.
  • Cooking tracks local absorbed energy, not temperature. It omits heat conduction, evaporation, and detailed melting physics. Dark food colours are not proof of scorching; light colours are not proof that bacteria survived.
  • Frozen patches use a deliberately simplified increase in absorption after a model threshold. This illustrates feedback, not a calibrated thawing time.
  • The turntable makes one revolution per 12 virtual seconds. Average power is applied continuously here; many real ovens cycle power.
  • Part 4 uses explanatory drawings, not a field solver or a real-oven safety test.

Suggested questions

  • Before explaining rotation: “The pattern did not change. What did?”
  • “Which point on a centred plate travels the shortest distance?”
  • “If everything gets equally little energy, is ‘even’ the same as ‘cooked’?”
  • “What did you hold constant to make your comparison fair?”
  • “When frequency doubled, what had to happen to wavelength?”
  • “What can this simulation tell us—and what can it not tell us?”

Cross-module thawing question

“Warm water would transfer heat faster. Why does the safety advice say cold?”

For the USDA cold-water method: use a leak-proof package submerged in cold tap water, change the water every 30 minutes, and cook immediately after thawing. Cold water is not a guarantee that the surface remains below 40°F throughout. Safe handling and prompt cooking still matter. Cook immediately after microwave thawing too.
