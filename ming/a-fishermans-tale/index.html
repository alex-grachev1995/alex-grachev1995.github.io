A Fisherman's Tale

In the 1960s, Peru's fishing industry thrived on a seemingly endless supply of anchovies. Each day, boats returned to shore laden with fish, which were the lifeblood of coastal communities in the arid region. However, in 1972, the catch plummeted, the fish vanished, and seabirds died off. Local fishermen noticed the ocean water was unusually warm and the air was humid—conditions they had seen before around Christmastime. They called this recurring phenomenon "El Niño." Today, scientists understand this is the warm phase of a larger climate pattern known as the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a cycle with impacts that reach across the entire globe.

About the Little Fish

The Peruvian anchovy is a small, shimmering fish that thrives in the cool, nutrient-rich waters of the Pacific Ocean off the west coast of South America. Its main food source is phytoplankton—microscopic algae that get their energy from the sun. Like many marine species, the survival of the anchovy is highly dependent on specific environmental conditions. Because young anchovies must find food quickly after hatching to survive, their entire population is deeply connected to the large-scale ocean processes that ensure a plentiful supply of this food.

The Best Place to be an Anchovy

The coast of Peru is a perfect habitat for anchovies due to a process called upwelling. Typically, strong coastal winds push the warm surface water of the Pacific away from the shore. This allows cold, deep water, which is loaded with nutrients, to rise to the surface. When this nutrient-rich water is exposed to sunlight, it fuels massive blooms of phytoplankton, creating a robust food web that supports a huge anchovy population. However, this crucial pattern of wind and upwelling is disrupted every few years, threatening the base of the entire ecosystem.

Less Wind Puts Fish in Hot Water

During an El Niño event, the normal atmospheric pressure patterns over the Pacific Ocean change, causing the trade winds along the South American coast to weaken. With less wind to push surface water away, the process of upwelling is significantly reduced. As a result, the cold, nutrient-rich deep water no longer reaches the surface, causing the ocean temperature to become unusually warm. This warm water cannot support the phytoplankton blooms that anchovies depend on, leading to a collapse in their food supply and a sharp decline in their population. These changes are part of the global ENSO cycle, which alters weather patterns across the world.

Not Enough Fish in the Sea

By the early 1970s, Peru was harvesting enormous quantities of anchovies, severely reducing the population's resiliency—its ability to withstand environmental shocks. When the 1972 El Niño event warmed the waters and cut off their food supply, the already-weakened fish population could not cope, leading to a catastrophic collapse. The combination of intense overfishing and a natural climate event proved devastating. The anchovy numbers have never fully returned to their previous levels. To prevent a similar disaster, the Peruvian government now enforces strict fishing quotas, aiming to maintain a more resilient population that can better survive future El Niño events.
