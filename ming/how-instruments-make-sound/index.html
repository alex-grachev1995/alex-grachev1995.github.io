How Do Instruments Make Sound?
A journey through the physics of music

Every musical instrument creates sound waves — vibrations traveling through air. But what vibrates is different for every instrument.

Scientists classify instruments into five families based on what produces the initial vibration. Let's explore each one.

Key idea: Sound = vibration. The question is always: what's vibrating, and how does the instrument make that vibration loud enough to hear?

Strings Chordophones
A stretched string vibrates

A tight string is plucked, bowed, or struck. It vibrates, pushing air molecules back and forth to create sound waves.

But a string alone is nearly silent — it's too thin to push much air. So stringed instruments use a resonant body (like a guitar's hollow box or a violin's carved body) to amplify the vibration.

Pitch depends on: string length (shorter = higher), tension (tighter = higher), and thickness (thinner = higher). A guitarist changes pitch by pressing strings against frets, which shortens the vibrating length.
*Piano surprise: A piano has strings inside! When you press a key, a felt hammer strikes a steel string. It's classified as a string instrument, even though you play it with keys.

Air Columns Aerophones
A column of air vibrates inside a tube

Blow air into or across a tube, and the air column inside vibrates at a specific frequency. The tube acts as a resonator — it selects which frequencies are amplified.

The player changes the effective length of the vibrating air column to change pitch — by covering holes, pressing valves, or extending a slide.

Pitch depends on: the length of the vibrating air column. Longer column = lower pitch. A tuba is low because its tubing is very long (coiled up). A piccolo is high because it's short.
How the vibration starts: Different wind instruments start the vibration differently — buzzing lips (brass), a vibrating reed (clarinet, sax), or air blown across an edge (flute). But in all cases, it's the air COLUMN that resonates.

Membranes Membranophones
A stretched skin or membrane vibrates

A thin membrane (animal skin or synthetic material) is stretched tight over a frame. When struck, the membrane vibrates rapidly, pushing large amounts of air and creating powerful sound waves.

The hollow body beneath amplifies the sound, just like a guitar body amplifies string vibrations.

Whole Body Idiophones
The instrument's own material vibrates

No strings, no air column, no membrane — the instrument's own body vibrates when struck, shaken, or scraped. The material itself (metal, wood, glass) is the vibrating element.

These are often the oldest instruments in human history — sometimes nothing more than two rocks struck together.

Electronic Electrophones
Sound is generated or shaped by electricity

Some instruments generate sound entirely from electrical circuits — no acoustic vibration at the source at all. A synthesizer creates waveforms mathematically and sends them to a speaker.

Others, like the theremin, detect the player's hand position using electromagnetic fields and convert that to sound — without being touched.

Where Does the Electric Guitar Fit?
It bridges two worlds

The electric guitar is fascinating because it lives in two categories at once:

Chordophone
The strings vibrate — that's the source of the sound
↕
Electrophone
But amplification happens electronically, not acoustically

An acoustic guitar amplifies string vibrations with a hollow body (100% chordophone). An electric guitar converts string vibrations into an electrical signal via electromagnetic pickups, then amplifies electronically.

The material of the body barely matters for the sound — what matters is the pickups, the strings, and the electronics. You could make an electric guitar body out of a shovel handle and it would still work. (People have done this!)

The lesson's core insight: In acoustic instruments, the physical shape and material determine the sound. In electric instruments, the electronics determine the sound. That's why electric guitar tone can be shaped infinitely with pedals and amplifiers.

"What's vibrating, and how is that vibration made loud enough to hear?"
