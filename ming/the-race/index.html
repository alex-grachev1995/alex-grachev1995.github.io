🏁 THE RACE

Antarctica, 1911. Two teams. Roughly 1,450 km to the South Pole and back.
Two expeditions are camped on the edge of Antarctica, both aiming for the same spot: the geographic South Pole, which no human being has ever stood on.
One is British, led by Robert Falcon Scott. One is Norwegian, led by Roald Amundsen. Neither knows exactly what the other is doing.
You are going to make eight decisions about how to run an expedition like this. Every one is a real choice one of these two men actually made. There are no right answers marked anywhere — just decisions, and consequences you cannot see yet.
Distances: about 685 km across a flat ice shelf, then a 200 km climb up a glacier, then 560 km across a high plateau to the pole. Then all of it again, backwards.

THE UNCOMFORTABLE CONCLUSION
Amundsen wasn't braver. He was boring.
Read back through the reveal table and notice the pattern in the Norwegian column. Dogs, because dogs work. Fur, because the people who live in the Arctic wear fur. Skis, because everyone on the team had used them since childhood. Flags stretching kilometres either side of every depot, because a single marker can be missed. Eat the dogs, because it was in the plan from the first day. Take exactly the number of men the food was packed for.
Not one of those is heroic. Not one would make a good painting. Amundsen's expedition is genuinely a bit dull to read about, and that is precisely why it worked. He removed every place where courage might be required, because courage is what you need when the planning has failed.
And Scott was not a fool. His temperature predictions were impressively accurate — modern weather stations along his route confirm it. He did the research. The plan should have worked. What killed his team was a season that turns up roughly once every fifteen years, landing on the one attempt they had.
Which is the real lesson, and it is the same one you have met before: you cannot judge a decision by its outcome. Scott made some genuinely poor choices — the fifth man, the single cairns, man-hauling — and he also got catastrophically unlucky. Amundsen made better choices and got an ordinary season. Untangling those two things is the whole job.
One last detail. Scott's team hauled roughly 14 kg of rock samples with them off the glacier — fossil plant remains, collected for science, dragged across a plateau by exhausted men. Those fossils were Glossopteris: leaves from a temperate forest. They are part of how we know Antarctica used to be green.
