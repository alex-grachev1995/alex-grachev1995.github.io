Every atom has a nucleus at the center made of protons (positive charge) and neutrons (no charge). Around the nucleus, electrons (negative charge) form a cloud. What makes one element different from another is the number of protons — hydrogen has 1, helium has 2, iron has 26, uranium has 92.

What's the problem of having protons inside a nucleus?
Protons are all positively charged. You know from magnets that same poles repel. So imagine 92 positive charges all crammed into a space smaller than a trillionth of a centimeter. What force is trying to destroy every nucleus?

The electromagnetic force pushes all protons apart because they're all positively charged. This repulsion gets stronger the more protons you pack together — which is why heavy atoms (with many protons) are less stable than medium-sized ones. Without something else holding them together, no nucleus with more than 1 proton could exist.

How are protons held together?
If protons constantly push each other apart, there MUST be something stronger holding them together — otherwise atoms couldn't exist. What is this force?
+
STRONG FORCE
+
The strong nuclear force is the most powerful force in nature — about 100× stronger than electromagnetism. But it has a critical limitation: it only works at incredibly tiny distances (about the width of a single proton, ~10⁻¹⁵ meters). At any distance larger than that, it drops to zero. That's why you never feel it — your hand is trillions of times too far from anything for the strong force to reach. Inside the nucleus, though, it overpowers the electromagnetic repulsion and acts like "nuclear glue."

Iron — The King of Stability
Not all nuclei are equally stable. Some atoms hold together more tightly than others. There's one element that sits at the absolute peak of nuclear stability — its nucleus is packed together more efficiently than any other. Understanding WHERE it sits on the periodic table is the key to understanding both fission AND fusion.
Binding energy per nucleon — how tightly each particle is held. Iron (Fe) sits at the peak.
Iron (Fe-56) has the most tightly-packed nucleus of all elements. On the "binding energy" graph, it sits at the very top. This means: atoms lighter than iron can release energy by fusing together (moving UP the curve toward iron). Atoms heavier than iron can release energy by splitting apart (also moving UP the curve toward iron). Iron itself is at the peak — you can't get energy out of it either way.

Two hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium + energy

Fusion = forcing small, light nuclei together to form a bigger one. The product is higher on the binding energy curve (closer to iron), meaning it's more stable — and the "leftover" energy is released. Inside the Sun, hydrogen atoms fuse into helium at 15 million °C. This is also the principle behind hydrogen bombs — which are far more powerful than fission bombs because fusion releases more energy per kilogram.

What is the force inside of an atomic bomb?
Uranium-235 has 92 protons and 143 neutrons — a massive, overstuffed nucleus barely held together. When just one extra neutron hits it, it's like the last drop that overflows a glass. One neutron hits uranium → splits into two medium atoms + 2-3 more neutrons + energy

Fission = splitting a heavy, unstable nucleus into two medium-sized pieces. The products are closer to iron on the binding energy curve (more stable), so energy is released. Crucially, each split also releases 2-3 extra neutrons. If those neutrons hit OTHER uranium-235 atoms, they split too — releasing MORE neutrons — creating a chain reaction. Uncontrolled = bomb. Controlled with rods that absorb excess neutrons = power plant.

Which needs more energy to get started?
Both fission and fusion release energy — but they both need a push to start. Think about it: in fusion, you're trying to force two positively-charged nuclei close enough for the strong force to grab them. In fission, you just need to destabilize an already-overstuffed nucleus. Which "push" is bigger?

Fusion needs FAR more energy to start. You must heat hydrogen to ~150 million °C — hotter than the center of the Sun — to force nuclei past their electromagnetic repulsion and close enough for the strong force to grab them. Fission just needs a single neutron to tip an already-unstable uranium nucleus over the edge. That's why we've had fission reactors since the 1950s, but fusion power is still in development — containing plasma at 150 million degrees is an extraordinary engineering challenge.

Nature's Own Nuclear Reactor
Humans built the first nuclear reactor in 1942. But nature beat us by about 1.7 billion years. In West Africa, scientists discovered evidence that a uranium deposit once sustained a natural fission chain reaction — all by itself, with no human involvement.
The Oklo uranium deposit in Gabon, Africa — site of a natural fission reactor 1.7 billion years ago
At Oklo in Gabon, groundwater seeped into a concentrated uranium deposit and acted as a "moderator" — slowing neutrons to the right speed for sustaining a chain reaction. It ran intermittently for hundreds of thousands of years. It can't happen today because Earth's uranium has decayed: 1.7 billion years ago, U-235 made up ~3.7% of natural uranium (enough to sustain fission). Today it's only 0.7% — too dilute for a natural chain reaction.
